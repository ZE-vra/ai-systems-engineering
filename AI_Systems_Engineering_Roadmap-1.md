# AI Systems Engineering: Six-Year Practical Roadmap

**Planning period:** 15 October 2026 – 15 October 2032  
**Primary learning workspace:** `ai-systems-engineering`  
**Primary programming language for the first two years:** Python  
**Long-term direction:** AI systems engineering and architecture, with a pathway into machine learning, deep learning, and research and development.

> This is a working plan, not a promise that a title, job, breakthrough, or wealth will arrive on a particular date. The dates create direction. Demonstrated ability decides when to advance.

---

## 1. The definite purpose

I want to become capable of designing, building, understanding, testing, and improving complex intelligent systems—not merely connecting an AI API to an application. I want to understand the software and infrastructure underneath those systems, the mathematics and algorithms behind machine learning, and how to investigate whether a new approach works.

Over time, I also want to use those abilities to solve real problems and create useful products. Technical skill and financial outcomes are related, but they are not the same thing: a product must solve a problem people care about, reach those people, and be sustainable. Learning alone does not guarantee wealth.

### What I am aiming to be able to do by the end of six years

I should be able to:

1. Take an unclear problem and turn it into requirements, a small design, and a practical implementation plan.
2. Write and maintain readable Python code, test it, debug it, and explain my decisions.
3. Design databases and APIs and understand how an application behaves under errors, load, and changing requirements.
4. Understand enough about operating systems, networks, concurrency, databases, and distributed systems to reason about the infrastructure beneath AI products.
5. Train and evaluate conventional machine-learning models, understand neural networks mathematically and practically, and use a deep-learning framework such as PyTorch.
6. Measure an AI system rather than judging it by a few impressive examples. Build evaluation datasets, choose metrics, examine failure cases, and compare alternatives.
7. Build AI systems that combine models with data, tools, state, workflows, and reliable execution—without assuming that an LLM call is the whole system.
8. Read technical papers, reproduce a manageable experiment, document discrepancies, and test a small extension where appropriate.
9. Validate whether a system solves a real problem before spending months polishing it.

I am **not** promising to master every area of computer science, train a frontier-scale model alone, or become a senior architect automatically in six years. I am building the foundations for increasingly difficult work.

## 2. How to use this roadmap

- Work on **one main learning resource at a time**. Do not collect ten courses and start all of them.
- The current task is always the task listed in the latest progress note, not the whole six-year plan.
- Treat a week as a target, not a deadline. If a concept is not understood, repeat the week or split it into two.
- Do not move forward because a video or chapter has ended. Move forward when the completion test at the end of the section is satisfied.
- Keep the projects small enough to finish. Finish a plain, tested project before adding a dashboard, AI features, or elaborate architecture.
- Keep large independent applications—such as `ai_editor`—in their own repositories. Use `ai-systems-engineering` for learning exercises, notes, experiments, and smaller portfolio projects.
- Review this plan every six months. Technology changes; core programming, mathematics, data, systems, and experimental thinking remain useful.

## 3. A practical study system

### Normal week

With work and university commitments, aim initially for **12–16 genuinely focused hours per week**. If more time is genuinely available without sacrificing sleep, health, school, or responsibilities, additional study is optional. A steady 12 focused hours beats a short burst followed by burnout.

A workable weekly pattern:

- **Three learning/practice sessions:** 90–120 minutes each.
- **One problem-solving session:** 90–120 minutes, with no tutorial open for at least part of it.
- **One project session:** 2–3 hours.
- **One review session:** 30–45 minutes to test yourself, write a weekly note, and plan the next week.
- Keep at least one lighter period or rest day. A missed day is not a reason to double the next day’s workload.

### If a four-hour session is realistically available

1. 45 minutes: read a small section or work through an example.
2. 60 minutes: type, run, and change the examples yourself.
3. 15 minutes: break.
4. 75 minutes: complete exercises or build the project without copying a full solution.
5. 30 minutes: mathematics or computer-science fundamentals.
6. 15 minutes: test, commit, and write a progress note.

This is a menu, not a requirement for every day. Protect adequate sleep and recovery. If the schedule leaves too little time for sleep, reduce study hours rather than trying to win by exhaustion.

### The learning loop for every chapter or topic

Use this loop instead of reading passively:

1. **Preview:** Write down what the topic is supposed to help you do.
2. **Learn:** Read a short section and study the examples.
3. **Recreate:** Type the examples yourself; do not merely copy and paste.
4. **Predict:** Before running code, predict its output or behaviour.
5. **Practise:** Complete the exercises.
6. **Change it:** Modify the example to solve a slightly different problem.
7. **Explain:** Describe what it does and why it works in your own words.
8. **Test and debug:** Deliberately try an edge case or make a small mistake and diagnose it.
9. **Record:** Write three to eight useful notes, then commit your work to Git.

### Definition of done

Mark a topic complete only when most of these are true:

- I can explain the main idea without reading the explanation aloud.
- I can write a small example from a blank file.
- I can solve two or three changed exercises without copying a solution.
- I can identify at least one common mistake and explain how to debug it.
- I can describe when the technique is useful and when it is not.
- My work is saved, named clearly, and committed where appropriate.

If I cannot do these yet, I am not failing. I have discovered what needs more practice.

### How to use AI while learning

- Try the problem yourself for 15–25 minutes first, unless you are completely blocked by setup.
- Ask for an explanation, a small hint, or a simpler example before asking for a full solution.
- If you inspect a suggested solution, close it and recreate the idea independently afterwards.
- Run the code. Test normal inputs, edge cases, and incorrect inputs.
- Ask: “What assumptions does this code make? What could fail? How would I test it?”
- Do not accept explanations merely because they sound confident. Verify important details against the book, documentation, or observed behaviour.
- Never put passwords, API keys, private data, or credentials into a public repository or an AI prompt.

Useful request to give an AI tutor:

> I am learning [topic]. Do not give me the full solution yet. Ask what I have tried, explain the relevant concept in plain language, and give me one hint at a time. After I solve it, review my approach, point out likely edge cases, and give me a similar exercise to do independently.

# YEAR 1 — Programming and Computer Science Foundations

**Dates:** October 2026–October 2027  
**Primary language:** Python  
**Primary book:** _Python Crash Course_ by Eric Matthes, starting from Chapter 1 and proceeding in the order of the edition you own.  
**Year-one goal:** become able to write small-to-medium programs independently, organize them sensibly, test and debug them, use Git with confidence, work with SQL, and finish one database-backed application.

**Pacing rule:** a row may need two weeks. That is normal. Do not sacrifice understanding to stay on schedule.

### Python mini-project: Personal Learning Tracker (first version)

Build a terminal programme that lets you:

- add a learning task or topic;
- list all tasks;
- mark a task complete;
- filter tasks by status or subject;
- save the tasks to a file;
- load them when the programme starts;
- handle empty input and missing/corrupt files sensibly.

Start with a simple version. Do not add an API, database, login system, AI features, or graphical interface yet.

**Completion test:** I can describe how data flows through the program, add a new feature without rewriting everything, and demonstrate that saved data survives closing and reopening the program.

## Phase 2 — Finish the book’s project sections (approximately weeks 13–26)

Work through the remaining project-based chapters in _Python Crash Course_ in order. Depending on the edition, these cover a game, data visualisation, and/or a web application. Follow the actual table of contents of your copy.

For each project:

1. Follow the project in small sections and run it frequently.
2. Before moving on, explain what the new code does.
3. Change at least two things yourself rather than leaving the tutorial example untouched.
4. Record one bug you encountered and how you diagnosed it.
5. Write a brief README with setup and run instructions.
6. Commit completed milestones.

Do not worry if the book’s project code feels much larger than the early chapters. The point is to practise connecting concepts over multiple files, not to memorise every line.

**Checkpoint after the book:** make a small variation on one project without following the exact instructions. If that proves too difficult, revisit the relevant chapters and try again.

## Phase 3 — Algorithms, data structures, and problem-solving (approximately weeks 27–36)

**Main resource:** _A Common-Sense Guide to Data Structures and Algorithms, Second Edition_ by Jay Wengrow. Read slowly and implement examples in Python.

Study, in order:

1. What an algorithm is and how to compare approaches.
2. Linear search and binary search; what makes binary search possible.
3. Basic sorting algorithms and the idea of measuring growth as input size increases.
4. Arrays/lists and their common operations.
5. Hash tables/dictionaries and why average lookup can be fast.
6. Stacks and queues.
7. Recursion and the importance of a stopping condition.
8. Trees and graphs at an introductory level, if the book covers them in your edition.
9. Big-O notation as a way to describe growth, not as a magic score for every program.

For every data structure, write a short Python example and note the operations it makes easy or expensive. You do not need to implement every sophisticated structure from scratch before building useful software.

**Practice routine:** solve a few beginner exercises per week. After reading a solution, close it and solve a similar problem independently. Keep the emphasis on reasoning, not on racing other learners.

**Checkpoint:** explain linear search versus binary search, implement both, state their basic time-growth difference, and explain why binary search requires appropriately ordered data.

## Phase 4 — SQL and databases (approximately weeks 37–44)

**Main practice resource:** [SQLBolt](https://sqlbolt.com/). Follow the introductory lessons and do the interactive exercises.

Study:

- tables, rows, columns, and data types;
- `SELECT`, `WHERE`, `ORDER BY`, and limits;
- `INSERT`, `UPDATE`, and `DELETE`;
- aggregate functions such as `COUNT` and `AVG`;
- primary keys and foreign keys;
- `JOIN`s;
- constraints and the reasons for using them;
- basic indexes and what problem they solve;
- transactions at a basic level;
- the difference between storing data in a file and storing it in a relational database.

Begin with SQLite for a small local application. Later, in Year 2, learn PostgreSQL for more realistic server-side work.

**Mini-project:** convert the Personal Learning Tracker from file storage to SQLite. Tables might include `topics`, `tasks`, and `study_sessions`. Decide what each table represents before writing the SQL.

**Checkpoint:** create the tables, insert records, query related data with a join, update a record, and explain primary/foreign keys in plain language.

## Phase 5 — Engineering habits and Year 1 capstone (approximately weeks 45–52)

Practise the following consistently:

- Git status, diff, log, branches, local merges, merge conflicts, and recovery commands used carefully;
- readable names and small functions;
- automated tests for important behaviour;
- clear error messages and appropriate exception handling;
- a README with purpose, setup, usage, examples, and known limitations;
- a `.gitignore` that excludes virtual environments, caches, secrets, and generated files;
- small commits that describe a meaningful change;
- not committing passwords, tokens, or private information.

### Year 1 capstone: Study and Project Tracker

Build a terminal application backed by SQLite. It should:

- create and list topics and tasks;
- mark tasks complete;
- record a study session and duration;
- show a simple summary by topic or week;
- reject invalid input cleanly;
- keep data after the app closes;
- include tests for key functions;
- have a README and a clean Git history.

You may build a similar application around another real problem, but keep the scope equivalent. No AI feature is required.

### Year 1 gate — Do not advance just because the year ended

I should be able to do most of the following without following a tutorial line by line:

- write a small multi-file Python program using functions and appropriate classes;
- read and write files and handle expected errors;
- write and run basic automated tests;
- use lists, dictionaries, and other common structures appropriately;
- solve basic searching/filtering problems and explain simple complexity differences;
- write SQL queries involving filtering, aggregation, and joins;
- use Git branches and make a normal merge; understand the basic steps of resolving a conflict;
- explain and demonstrate my capstone to another person.

If two or three items remain weak, choose those as the next month’s focus before adding more tools.

# YEAR 2 — Software Engineering and Backend Systems

**Dates:** October 2027–October 2028  
**Goal:** build software that is not just working on my laptop, but is structured, tested, secure at a basic level, observable, and reproducible.

## Quarter 1 — Python project structure and testing

Study:

- modules and packages;
- virtual environments and dependency management;
- type hints and clear interfaces;
- test organisation and fixtures;
- unit tests versus integration tests;
- debugging and logging;
- configuration via environment variables;
- separating application logic from input/output code.

**Project:** refactor the Year 1 application into a well-organised Python package with tests. Document how another person can set it up from a clean environment.

**Gate:** another developer can follow the README, install dependencies, run tests, and understand where the key logic lives.

## Quarter 2 — HTTP and APIs

Learn the practical basics of:

- clients, servers, requests, and responses;
- HTTP methods and status codes;
- JSON;
- URL paths, query parameters, and request bodies;
- input validation and useful error responses;
- API documentation;
- authentication versus authorization;
- environment variables and safe secret handling.

**Main framework:** FastAPI. Use its official tutorial as the step-by-step reference: https://fastapi.tiangolo.com/tutorial/.

Do not learn FastAPI and Django simultaneously. Learn one framework well enough to create, test, and maintain a complete application.

**Project:** expose the Learning Tracker’s core operations through an API. Write tests for success cases, missing records, invalid inputs, and unexpected conditions.

## Quarter 3 — PostgreSQL and reliable data handling

Learn:

- relational schema design and relationships;
- PostgreSQL basics;
- constraints, indexes, and query plans at an introductory level;
- transactions and what can go wrong when concurrent requests modify data;
- schema migrations;
- database connection settings and pooling concepts;
- backups and restoration;
- pagination and filtering for API endpoints.

**Project:** move the API’s persistent data from SQLite to PostgreSQL. Practise restoring from a backup, rather than merely creating a backup file.

## Quarter 4 — Testing, security, deployment, and delivery

Learn:

- unit, integration, and end-to-end test concepts;
- continuous integration, so tests run automatically on changes;
- Docker basics: image, container, ports, volumes, and environment variables;
- structured logging and error reporting;
- dependency updates and vulnerability awareness;
- input validation, access control, and avoiding SQL injection;
- basic web security concepts and the OWASP Top 10 overview;
- a deployment workflow, or a reproducible local setup if free hosting is unavailable.

**Year 2 capstone:** a documented API-backed application with a relational database, automated tests, environment-based configuration, logs, security basics, and a repeatable setup. A live public deployment is useful but does not replace tests and documentation.

### Optional Go refresher

Only after Python, API, testing, and database work is reasonably comfortable, spend 4–6 weeks revisiting _Learning Go_ by Jon Bodner. Build one small command-line tool or HTTP service. Go is a useful second language to understand, but it is not a prerequisite for advancing toward ML. Do not split every week between two languages.

### Year 2 gate

I can explain the path of a request through my API, trace a failed request from logs, write a test reproducing a bug, design a basic relational schema, describe how authentication differs from authorization, and show how to set up the project from scratch.

# YEAR 3 — Computer Systems and Reliability

**Dates:** October 2028–October 2029  
**Goal:** understand the environment in which software runs and learn to reason about failure, performance, and distributed behaviour.

## Quarter 1 — Linux, processes, and operating systems

Study:

- files and directories, permissions, and processes;
- environment variables and shell pipelines;
- processes versus threads;
- memory, allocation, and virtual memory concepts;
- scheduling and blocking;
- filesystems and I/O;
- concurrency problems and race conditions.

**Main resource:** _Operating Systems: Three Easy Pieces_ (OSTEP), using the free online book and choosing its introductory material in a sensible sequence: https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~remzi/OSTEP/.

**Practice:** inspect running processes, run small programs that read/write files, and write a tiny concurrent example. Explain the behaviour before and after changing it.

## Quarter 2 — Networking

Study:

- IP addresses and ports;
- DNS and how names resolve;
- TCP versus UDP at a conceptual level;
- HTTP requests and responses;
- TLS and what HTTPS provides;
- latency, bandwidth, timeouts, and retries;
- what a reverse proxy does;
- how a browser or client reaches an API.

**Project:** draw and document the request path from a client to your API, application, database, and back. Use logs and command-line tools to investigate a deliberately introduced connection or timeout problem.

## Quarter 3 — Databases and data-intensive applications

Study:

- indexes and query plans;
- transaction isolation and concurrency at a conceptual level;
- caching and invalidation trade-offs;
- replication and partitioning concepts;
- consistency and availability trade-offs;
- durable storage, backup, and recovery;
- queues and asynchronous background jobs;
- idempotency, retries, and duplicate work.

**Main book:** _Designing Data-Intensive Applications_ by Martin Kleppmann (use the edition available to you). Read thoughtfully; it is a book to work through and discuss, not skim quickly.

## Quarter 4 — Distributed-systems project

Build a modest background-job system. It should accept tasks, process them separately from the request, record status, retry a deliberately simulated temporary failure, avoid uncontrolled repeated work, and expose logs or a status query.

Do not imitate a giant cloud service. A small, clear experiment that shows what happens during a failure is better.

### Year 3 gate

I can explain the difference between a process and a thread, trace an HTTP request, explain why timeouts and retries are necessary, demonstrate a database backup restoration, identify a likely source of slow queries, and describe what my job system does when a worker fails.

# YEAR 4 — Machine Learning and AI System Foundations

**Dates:** October 2029–October 2030  
**Goal:** learn how models are trained and evaluated before attempting complex deep-learning systems.

## Quarter 1 — Data work and the mathematics needed for ML

Practise Python with NumPy, pandas, and basic plotting. Learn how to inspect a dataset, detect missing or suspicious values, summarise distributions, and keep a record of data-cleaning decisions.

Mathematics to study in parallel:

- vectors, matrices, and matrix multiplication;
- functions and graphs;
- mean, variance, and standard deviation;
- probability and conditional probability;
- derivatives and the meaning of a gradient;
- sampling and why data collection affects results.

Use an introductory resource such as Khan Academy for probability/statistics and MIT OpenCourseWare 18.06 for linear algebra when ready: https://www.khanacademy.org/math/statistics-probability and https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06sc-linear-algebra-fall-2011/.

## Quarter 2 — Classical machine learning

**Main course:** Google’s Machine Learning Crash Course: https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/crash-course/.

Study in a measured order:

1. supervised versus unsupervised learning;
2. features, labels, and data splitting;
3. regression and classification;
4. loss functions and optimisation intuition;
5. train, validation, and test sets;
6. overfitting and generalisation;
7. baseline models and suitable metrics;
8. class imbalance and data leakage;
9. clustering and dimensionality reduction at an introductory level.

Use scikit-learn for practical training and evaluation. Begin with small, understandable datasets. Never judge a model solely from its accuracy on the same data it learned from.

**Project:** choose a modest prediction problem, define a baseline, split the data correctly, train at least two reasonable approaches, compare metrics, and inspect examples where the model fails.

## Quarter 3 — Neural network foundations

Study:

- neurons, layers, weights, and biases;
- activation functions;
- loss functions;
- gradient descent;
- backpropagation and the chain rule;
- learning rates and optimisation;
- training versus inference;
- why neural networks overfit;
- reproducible experiments and random seeds.

Implement a very small model with NumPy to understand the mathematics, then use a framework for practical work. Do not spend months implementing a complete deep-learning framework.

## Quarter 4 — AI system design and evaluation

Learn the difference between a model and the whole system around it. Explore:

- the user problem and model choice;
- data preparation and provenance;
- baseline and evaluation dataset design;
- embeddings and retrieval as measurable components;
- structured outputs and tool interfaces;
- application state and workflow control;
- latency, cost, privacy, and reliability;
- failure analysis and safe fallback behaviour.

**Project:** build a small intelligent application. It must include a baseline, an evaluation set, explicit metrics, logged failures, and a short report explaining what works and what does not. Using a hosted model is allowed here, but the learning goal is to evaluate the whole system, not simply connect the API.

### Year 4 gate

I can explain train/validation/test separation, train a baseline model, choose a suitable metric, identify overfitting or data leakage, explain gradient descent in simple terms, and present evidence for an AI system’s strengths and weaknesses.

# YEAR 5 — Deep Learning and Advanced AI Engineering

**Dates:** October 2030–October 2031  
**Goal:** understand and experiment with modern neural networks, model adaptation, and more sophisticated AI systems.

## Quarter 1 — PyTorch fundamentals

Use the official beginner tutorials: https://docs.pytorch.org/tutorials/beginner/basics/.

Work through tensors, datasets and dataloaders, model definition, automatic differentiation, optimisation, and saving/loading models. Complete a small training task and explain what each part of the training loop does.

## Quarter 2 — Deep-learning architecture

Study with _Understanding Deep Learning_ by Simon J. D. Prince, using the chapters that match your current mathematics and experiments. The publisher page is https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262048644/understanding-deep-learning/.

Topics to build toward:

- multilayer neural networks;
- convolutional networks or sequence models where relevant;
- embeddings and representation learning;
- attention and transformer components;
- loss functions and optimisation choices;
- regularisation and generalisation;
- training curves and diagnosing failed experiments.

Use one book as the central path. Treat _Dive into Deep Learning_ at https://d2l.ai/ as a supplementary reference rather than a second full course to pursue at the same time.

## Quarter 3 — Transformers and model adaptation

Learn the purpose of tokenisation, embeddings, attention, positional information, and transformer blocks. Reproduce small educational experiments where feasible.

Explore fine-tuning or adaptation of an appropriately small, available model. Check the model’s licence, data requirements, compute requirements, and evaluation method before choosing it. A small, carefully evaluated experiment is preferable to attempting to train a large model without adequate resources.

Study inference trade-offs: model size, memory, throughput, latency, batching, and quantisation concepts. Use measurements instead of assuming one method is always better.

## Quarter 4 — Research reproduction and evaluated AI system

Pick one manageable paper, report, or established method with enough detail to reproduce. Write down:

- the question being investigated;
- the original method and expected result;
- data and evaluation conditions;
- your implementation choices;
- the result you obtained;
- differences from the original and plausible reasons;
- limitations and the next experiment.

**Project:** an AI system that combines at least one learned model with application logic and includes a repeatable evaluation process. It must have documented failure cases and an explanation of why the chosen design is appropriate.

### Year 5 gate

I can train a neural network with PyTorch, explain the training loop and gradient-based optimisation, monitor learning curves, evaluate model errors, adapt a small model or run a small training experiment, and document an experiment so someone else can understand what I did.

# YEAR 6 — Specialization, Research, and Product Capability

**Dates:** October 2031–October 2032  
**Goal:** choose one area for depth, then demonstrate it with a serious, documented project. This is not the year to start every possible specialty.

At the start of Year 6, review your strongest interests, the work you have actually enjoyed, your mathematics progress, project results, and available tools. Choose **one primary track** and one supporting skill.

## Track A — AI systems architecture and intelligent agents

Focus on the complete system: model selection, state, tool interfaces, planning, workflow control, memory/retrieval, evaluation, error handling, permissions, observability, and recovery. Compare deterministic workflows with agentic ones and measure when additional autonomy helps or hurts.

**Capstone idea:** a bounded AI workflow that completes a real multi-step task with explicit constraints, evaluation cases, logs, recovery behaviour, and human approval for consequential actions.

## Track B — Machine learning and deep learning engineering

Focus on data pipelines, training and fine-tuning, experiment design, reproducibility, model evaluation, performance, and deployment.

**Capstone idea:** a trained or adapted model for a clearly defined task, compared against a baseline with meaningful metrics and failure analysis.

## Track C — AI infrastructure and platform engineering

Focus on model serving, batch jobs, resource use, model versioning, evaluation pipelines, latency, throughput, storage, monitoring, and operational reliability.

**Capstone idea:** a small model-serving and evaluation platform with documented performance measurements, versioned models, automated tests, and recovery procedures.

## Track D — AI research engineering

Focus on reading papers, implementing methods, reproducing baselines, running controlled ablations, maintaining experimental logs, and communicating results clearly. Build enough mathematics to understand the chosen research area rather than trying to study all advanced mathematics at once.

**Capstone idea:** reproduce a manageable result and add a small, well-motivated extension or systematic comparison. A negative result can still be useful if the experiment is sound and reported honestly.

## Final capstone requirements

Whichever track I choose, the finished work should include:

- a clear problem statement and intended user;
- an explanation of why the problem matters;
- architecture or method diagram where useful;
- setup and reproduction instructions;
- tests or evaluation procedures;
- results with limitations and failure cases;
- design trade-offs and rejected alternatives;
- a short demonstration;
- a retrospective explaining what I would improve next.

### Year 6 gate

I can show a coherent body of work, explain the technical choices, reproduce the results, reason about failure modes, and describe what evidence would be needed to improve it. The project should demonstrate real capability, not just a large quantity of code.

You do not need to create all these folders now. Add one when there is actual work to put in it.

For new work, a safe ordinary workflow is:

```bash
git status
git switch -c topic/short-description
# Make and test a small change.
git diff
git add path/to/changed-file
git commit -m "Describe the change"
# Switch to the correct base branch and merge when ready.
# Push after confirming the repository is in the expected state.
```

This is an example, not a universal set of commands. Before merges or recovery operations, inspect `git status` and confirm which branch you are on. Be careful with reset and restore commands; some uses can discard work.

Use [Pro Git](https://git-scm.com/book/en/v2) as the reference for branches, merges, remote branches, conflicts, and recovery. Learn `git restore`, `git revert`, and reset operations by understanding the consequences—not by memorising commands from a list.

## 7. Product and financial thinking throughout the six years

The technical path and the product path should support each other, but they must not be confused.

Maintain an `opportunity-log.md` or a private notebook with one entry per problem:

- Who experiences the problem?
- What do they do today?
- How often does it happen and how costly or frustrating is it?
- What evidence supports the claim beyond my own opinion?
- Can I test demand with a small conversation, mock-up, manual service, or prototype?
- What is the smallest useful solution?
- Would anyone use it repeatedly or pay for it? What evidence do I have?
- What did I learn, and should I continue, change direction, or stop?

In Years 1–2, choose small problems close to your own experience and build simple tools. In Years 3–4, start measuring whether others find the tools useful. In Years 5–6, combine technical depth with stronger validation and delivery.

Do not spend months building a complicated system solely because the technology is exciting. Evidence from users should influence what gets built next. Do not assume a technically advanced product will automatically become a profitable business.

## 9. What to do when stuck

1. Read the error message carefully and identify the first relevant line.
2. Reproduce the problem with the smallest possible example.
3. Check the current file, variable values, inputs, and assumptions.
4. Search the book or official documentation for the specific concept involved.
5. Ask an AI tutor for one hint or an explanation of the error, not an unexamined replacement project.
6. Change one thing at a time and rerun the test.
7. Write down the cause and the fix once the problem is understood.

Being stuck for a while is expected. Repeatedly changing many things without testing them is what this process is designed to prevent.

## 10. The exact starting plan

**Current focus:** _Python Crash Course_, from the first chapter, while retaining the `ai-systems-engineering` repository as the overall learning workspace.

### Before the first study session

- Confirm which edition of _Python Crash Course_ you own and use its chapter order.
- Open the book at Chapter 1.
- Open the repository in VS Code.
- Run `git status` before making changes.
- Create a small working directory for the exercises; keep the book’s examples separate from your own experiments.
- Confirm that Python runs from the terminal and that you can run a `.py` file from VS Code.

### First week

**Session 1:** Set up the environment, run the first program, deliberately make a simple syntax mistake, read the error, and correct it. Write down the exact command used to run the program.

**Session 2:** Complete the exercises for the opening chapter. Do not rush through exercises just because they look easy. Make sure you can explain how you ran the program.

**Session 3:** Repeat the examples without copying them line by line. Change the output and filenames, then run them from the terminal.

**Session 4:** Practise reading error messages and locating the line that caused the problem. Write a short setup note that future-you can follow.

**Review session:** Close the book and explain how a Python file is run. Create one tiny program from a blank file, review `git status`, and commit the completed exercise if it is ready.

### The next three months

Proceed through the Year 1 Python sequence. The first target is not to become an expert in 90 days. It is to build a reliable practice habit, understand the foundations, complete exercises independently, and finish a small program whose behaviour you can explain.

If one chapter takes longer, let it take longer. If university examinations or work become demanding, reduce the number of sessions temporarily and return to the plan when the pressure passes. Do not restart from Chapter 1 after every interruption; use the weekly note to resume exactly where you stopped.

## Final principle

The goal is not to look busy or to finish the largest number of courses. The goal is to build durable capability. Learn a concept, practise it, build something with it, test it, explain it, and repeat. Let real problems and evidence guide the products you build. Let demonstrated competence—not a calendar date or a motivational quote—decide when you are ready for the next stage.
